Polybutylene Pipes: How to Spot Them, and Your Options (Galvanized, Copper and PEX)

By PipeMend Editorial · Published November 20, 2026 · Updated November 20, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: Polybutylene pipes are gray, blue or black flexible plastic supply lines, often stamped "PB2110," used in many U.S. homes built from the late 1970s to the mid-1990s. They have a long history of sudden leaks at the pipe and its fittings, so most plumbers recommend replacing them. When you repipe, PEX and copper are the usual choices. Old galvanized steel pipe is the other common problem material; it rusts inside and chokes water pressure.

Many homeowners first hear the word polybutylene from a home inspector or an insurance agent. This guide shows you how to identify it, why it fails, and how it compares with other pipe materials, so you can plan. When you're ready, PipeMend sends licensed plumbers for pipe repair and whole-house repiping with PEX or copper.

What are polybutylene pipes?

Polybutylene (PB) is a plastic resin that was widely used for water supply pipes because it was cheap, flexible and easy to install. It went into millions of homes, mobile homes and apartment buildings from the late 1970s to the mid-1990s. Over time, many systems failed. The pipe can become brittle and crack from the inside, and early plastic and metal crimp fittings were prone to leaks. Production stopped in the 1990s, and polybutylene is no longer used for new water lines.

How to tell if you have polybutylene pipes

  • Color: usually gray, sometimes blue or black. Outdoor service lines are often blue.
  • Markings: look for "PB2110" or "PB" printed on the pipe.
  • Feel: flexible plastic, often 1/2 to 1 inch across.
  • Fittings: gray plastic or copper or aluminum crimp rings.
  • Where to look: near the water heater, under sinks, in the basement or crawl space, and where the main line enters the house.

Don't rely on the pipe stubs under sinks alone. Some homes have copper stubs coming out of the wall with polybutylene behind it. A plumber or inspector can check in the attic, crawl space or at the water heater.

Why does polybutylene fail?

The material can break down over time, especially with chlorinated water and high water pressure. Failures often come without warning: a fitting lets go, or a pipe splits inside a wall or ceiling. Because leaks can be hidden, damage may spread before anyone sees it. Many insurers ask about polybutylene when writing a policy for an older home.

What are galvanized pipes?

Galvanized pipe is steel coated with zinc. It was standard for water lines before copper took over. After decades, the zinc wears away and the steel rusts from the inside, narrowing the opening. The City of Bloomington, Minnesota, says scale buildup in old galvanized pipe restricts flow and recommends replacing it with an approved alternative. That is why galvanized pipe is a top cause of low water pressure in older houses.

Lead and old plumbing

The EPA says homes built before 1986 are more likely to have lead pipes, fixtures and solder, and that lead service lines are typically the most significant source of lead in drinking water. It also warns that lead particles can attach to galvanized pipes and later enter the water. If your home is that old, test your water and ask your utility about your service line material.

What is PEX plumbing?

PEX (cross-linked polyethylene) is flexible plastic tubing used for hot and cold water. It comes in red, blue and white, connects with crimp, clamp or expansion fittings, and can run long distances in one piece. That means fewer joints and smaller wall openings during a repipe. It must be kept out of sunlight, and installers must use fittings rated for the specific PEX type.

What does a repipe involve?

  1. Inspection of pipe type, fixture count and access routes.
  2. Written plan and estimate, including the permit and drywall patching.
  3. New lines run through walls, attic or crawl space with small openings.
  4. Connections and pressure test, then flushing the new lines.
  5. Inspection and patching of openings.

Many homes are repiped in a few days, with water on outside working hours. Repiping is also a good time to add better freeze protection; see how to keep pipes from freezing. The American Red Cross recommends insulating pipes in unheated areas, and PEX's flexibility helps too.

Local note: Homes from the polybutylene era are common in fast-growing suburbs in Georgia, Virginia, Texas and Florida. Galvanized pipe is more likely in older housing in New York and Ohio.

When should you call a plumber?

  • You find gray or blue PB pipe, or "PB2110" markings.
  • Your insurer or inspector flagged your pipes.
  • You've had more than one leak in a year or two.
  • Water pressure is low throughout the house, or water is rusty.
  • You're remodeling and walls are already open.

Pipe type questions

Do I have to replace polybutylene pipes?

No law requires homeowners to remove it, but many plumbers and home inspectors recommend replacement because of its failure history, and some insurers ask about it or limit coverage. Replacing it on your schedule is usually cheaper than after a leak.

Can I just replace the polybutylene fittings?

Some homeowners replace failing fittings as a stopgap, but the pipe itself can still deteriorate. A full repipe is the lasting fix.

What is PEX plumbing?

PEX is cross-linked polyethylene, a flexible plastic tubing used for hot and cold water lines. It runs long distances with few fittings, which means fewer joints and smaller wall openings during a repipe.

Is copper or PEX better?

Both are widely used. PEX costs less to install and handles freezing better; copper is rigid, long-lived and handles heat and sunlight. Local code, water chemistry and budget decide.

Are galvanized pipes dangerous?

Old galvanized pipe rusts inside, which lowers pressure and can make water brown. The EPA notes that lead particles can attach to galvanized pipe surfaces, so testing your water is a good idea.
