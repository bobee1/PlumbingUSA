Short answer: How long does a water heater last? The U.S. Department of Energy says most storage tank water heaters last 10 to 15 years, and tankless water heaters can last more than 20 years. Hard water, high temperature settings and skipped maintenance shorten that. A tank that leaks from its body, makes rusty hot water or is past 10 years with repeated repairs is usually ready for replacement.

Water heaters tend to fail at the worst time, on a cold morning or right before guests arrive. Knowing your heater's age and the warning signs lets you replace it on your schedule instead of in an emergency. How long does a water heater last, by type?

TypeTypical lifeWhat wears it out
Gas tank10 to 15 years (DOE)Tank corrosion, sediment, burner and flue wear
Electric tank10 to 15 years (DOE)Tank corrosion, scale on elements
Heat pump (hybrid) tankTank life plus compressor and fan upkeepTank corrosion, dirty air filter, condensate drain clogs
TanklessMore than 20 years (DOE)Scale in the heat exchanger, ignition and sensor parts

These are typical ranges, not guarantees. Two identical heaters can last very different lengths of time depending on water quality and care.

How old is my water heater?

Look at the label on the side of the tank. The manufacture date is usually built into the serial number, often as a letter for the month and two digits for the year, or as the first four digits (year and week). Formats differ by brand, so check the manufacturer's website or call their support line with the serial number. If the heater has an install sticker or a permit card nearby, that tells you when it went in.

Signs your water heater is failing

  • Water around the base or rust streaks on the tank. See our water heater leaking guide.
  • Rusty or brown hot water while cold water runs clear.
  • Popping, rumbling or banging as the burner or elements run, a sign of heavy sediment.
  • Running out of hot water faster than it used to.
  • Water that never gets as hot even after resetting the thermostat.
  • A rotten-egg smell in hot water only, often a reaction with the anode rod.
  • Repeated repairs on an older unit.

What shortens a water heater's life?

Hard water. Calcium and magnesium settle as scale and sediment. The USGS classifies water above 120 mg/L as hard and above 180 mg/L as very hard, and much of the Southwest and Midwest falls in those ranges.

High temperature settings. Hotter water speeds corrosion and scale. The Department of Energy says 120°F is safe for most people and saves energy, and the CPSC urges the same setting to prevent scalds.

No maintenance. A spent anode rod lets the tank rust, and sediment overheats the tank bottom.

High water pressure and thermal expansion stress the tank and relief valve. See our guide to water pressure for normal ranges.

Location. Garages and attics with big temperature swings, and salty coastal air, are hard on heaters.

How to make a water heater last longer

  1. Flush sediment. The DOE suggests draining a quart of water from the tank every three months. Follow your manufacturer's instructions.
  2. Check the anode rod every few years and replace it when it is mostly eaten away.
  3. Set the temperature to 120°F.
  4. Test the T&P relief valve once a year.
  5. Insulate. The DOE says insulating an older tank can cut standby heat loss and save 7 to 16 percent on water heating costs.
  6. Treat hard water with a softener if scale is heavy.
  7. Descale tankless units on the maker's schedule.

Repair or replace a water heater?

Repair usually makes sense when the heater is well inside its expected life and a single part has failed, such as a thermocouple, element, thermostat or valve. Replacement usually makes sense when:

  • The tank body leaks.
  • The heater is near or past 10 to 15 years and needs a costly part.
  • Hot water stays rusty after the anode rod is replaced.
  • The heater is too small for your household.

What to consider when you replace it

A replacement is a chance to upgrade. ENERGY STAR says a labeled heat pump water heater can save a household of four about $550 a year compared with a standard electric heater, and the DOE says heat pump models can be two to three times more efficient. Tankless units suit homes that want endless hot water within a flow limit; compare them in our tankless vs. tank guide. Replacement usually needs a permit, and codes may require an expansion tank, drain pan or seismic straps.

Does the water heater warranty tell you how long it will last?

A tank's warranty, often 6, 9 or 12 years, is a rough guide to how long the maker expects the tank to hold. Longer-warranty models usually have thicker insulation, a larger or second anode rod, and better parts. A warranty doesn't guarantee that life, and it usually covers only the tank and parts, not labor or water damage. Keep your receipt and register the heater with the manufacturer so a claim is easier if the tank fails early.

Plan the replacement before it fails

If your heater is near the end of its expected life, a little planning saves money and stress. Measure the space and note the fuel type, the tank size and the venting. Decide whether you want the same type or an upgrade. Ask a plumber what code items your area will require, such as an expansion tank or a drain pan. Replacing it on a weekday, before it leaks, avoids emergency rates and water damage.

Local note: Very hard water in Arizona, Utah and Iowa wears tanks out faster, so flushing and anode checks matter more there. In Hawaii, many homes use solar water heaters, which have their own maintenance schedule.

When should you call a plumber?

  • The tank leaks, or water appears at the base.
  • Hot water is rusty or smells of rotten eggs.
  • The heater pops loudly or the relief valve discharges.
  • Your heater is past 10 years and needs a major part.
  • You want to switch to a heat pump or tankless model.
