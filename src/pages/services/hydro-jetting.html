Quick answer: Hydro jetting cleans a drain or sewer line with water at high pressure, scouring away grease, soap scale, sludge and tree roots that a snake leaves behind. A camera inspection comes first to make sure the pipe is sound. Jetting is the usual fix for main sewer line clogs that keep coming back.

What hydro jetting includes

  • Camera inspection before jetting to check pipe material, condition and the clog's location.
  • Access through an indoor or outdoor cleanout, or a roof vent when needed.
  • Nozzle choice: penetrating nozzles for blockages, rotating nozzles for grease, root-cutting nozzles for roots.
  • Jetting the full run of the main drain or sewer lateral, upstream and back.
  • Debris flushing to the city main or septic tank.
  • Camera check after to confirm a clean pipe and spot any damage.

When hydro jetting is the right call

Jetting is not the right call when the camera shows a collapsed, broken or badly offset pipe. Pressure can make that damage worse. Those lines need sewer line repair.

The hydro jetting process

  1. Locate the cleanout. The plumber finds the cleanout or best access point. If there is none, the toilet may be pulled, or a cleanout added.
  2. Run the camera. A sewer camera shows pipe material, joints, roots, sags and the blockage.
  3. Set up the jetter. Hose size, pressure and nozzle are matched to the pipe diameter and what is inside it.
  4. Jet the line. The nozzle pulls itself forward on rear-facing jets, scouring the walls, then is drawn back slowly to flush debris down the line.
  5. Re-inspect. A second camera pass confirms the pipe is clean and records its condition for you.
  6. Test flow. Fixtures are run together to confirm the drain keeps up.

Grease and roots: the two big causes

The EPA's Report to Congress on sewer overflows found grease to be the most common cause of reported sewer blockages, at 47 percent. Grease builds up in layers, so snaking it leaves most of the coating behind. Roots are the other big cause. The EPA notes that roots enter sewer lines through defects and openings. Jetting cuts them out, but the opening stays, so lines with heavy roots often need a spot repair or a pipe liner after jetting.

Homes, rentals and businesses

Most homes only need jetting when a main line problem shows up. Restaurants are different. Local sewer rules often require grease traps or interceptors, and kitchen lines may need jetting on a schedule to stay clear. For multi-unit buildings, one blocked main can back up into several units, so scheduled jetting is cheaper than emergency calls. See drain cleaning for single-fixture clogs.

What affects the cost of hydro jetting

  • Length and diameter of the line.
  • How easy the cleanout is to reach, or whether one must be added.
  • How heavy the grease, scale or root buildup is.
  • Camera inspection and a recorded video report.
  • After-hours timing for an active backup.

DIY or call a professional?

Hydro jetting is not a do-it-yourself job. Pressure washers with drain attachments do not have the flow to clean a sewer line, and high-pressure water can injure skin. Call a plumber for any main line clog.

Keeping lines clear after jetting

  • Keep grease, oil and food scraps out of the kitchen sink.
  • Flush only toilet paper.
  • Ask about root treatments or a liner if roots were the cause.
  • Plan a camera check every year or two on older cast iron or clay lines.
