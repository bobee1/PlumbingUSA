Quick answer: Backflow testing checks that the backflow preventer on your water line still stops dirty water from flowing back into the drinking water supply. A qualified tester attaches a gauge to the assembly's test cocks and checks each valve, then files a report with the water utility. Failed assemblies are repaired or replaced and retested.

How backflow happens

What backflow service includes

  • Annual testing with a calibrated differential pressure gauge.
  • Test reports filed with your water utility by its deadline.
  • Repair of check valves, springs, seals and relief valves.
  • Replacement of assemblies that can't be repaired.
  • New installation for irrigation, fire lines, boilers and businesses.
  • Freeze protection with insulated enclosures or seasonal draining.
  • Cross-connection surveys to find unprotected connections.

The backflow testing process

  1. Schedule. You share the notice from your utility and the assembly's location.
  2. Short shutoff. Water to the protected line is shut off briefly for the test.
  3. Test. A gauge is attached to the test cocks, and each check valve and relief valve is checked against required readings.
  4. Report. Results are recorded and filed with the water utility, and you get a copy.
  5. Repair if it fails. Worn parts are replaced, and the assembly is retested.

Why backflow testing is required

The EPA's cross-connection control manual says water suppliers must protect the public system from backflow hazards. That includes the testing, installation and ongoing operation of backflow prevention assemblies matched to the degree of hazard. An EPA fact sheet describes a real case: an irrigation line was connected to a public system without an approved assembly, irrigation water flowed back, and customers were told not to use their water. Your annual test is part of preventing that.

Backflow risks at home

Backflow isn't only a business issue. A garden hose left in a bucket of fertilizer, a pool or a sprayer can siphon back into household pipes if water pressure drops, for example during a water main break. That is why many codes require vacuum breakers on outdoor hose bibbs, a pressure vacuum breaker or other assembly on lawn sprinkler systems, and an air gap on dishwasher drains. Homes with boilers, wells tied to city water, or rainwater systems may need more protection. A plumber can check your home's connections.

What affects the cost of backflow service

  • Type and size of the assembly.
  • Number of assemblies on the property.
  • Repairs needed after a failed test.
  • Access, enclosures and freeze protection.
  • Permit and filing fees set by your utility.

DIY or call a professional?

Backflow testing must be done by a tester who meets your state and local requirements, using calibrated equipment, so it is not a DIY task. What you can do: know where your assembly is, protect it from freezing, and keep the utility's test notice so deadlines aren't missed.

Protect your backflow preventer

  • Insulate or drain outdoor assemblies before freezing weather.
  • Keep the assembly accessible, not buried in landscaping.
  • Don't remove or bypass a preventer; it is often required by law.
  • Test on schedule and keep copies of the reports.
