Quick answer: A water softener removes the calcium and magnesium that make water hard, using resin beads that trade those minerals for sodium or potassium. Installation means testing your water, sizing the unit, and installing it on the main line with a bypass valve, a drain and an overflow line. The USGS classifies water above 120 mg/L as hard and above 180 mg/L as very hard.

What water softener service includes

  • Water testing for hardness, and iron where needed.
  • Sizing by hardness, household size and daily water use.
  • Installation on the main line, with a bypass valve.
  • Drain and overflow lines with an air gap to the drain.
  • Programming of the control valve for your hardness and use.
  • Repair: control valves, injectors, brine lines and float assemblies.
  • Resin replacement and salt tank cleaning.
  • Replacement and haul-away of old units.

Hardness is caused mostly by calcium and magnesium, according to the USGS. The USGS also notes that the EPA has set no health standard for hardness, because those minerals aren't toxic. Softening is about protecting plumbing and appliances, and about how the water feels.

The water softener installation process

  1. Test. The plumber measures hardness and checks for iron, which affects the choice of unit.
  2. Size. Capacity is matched to hardness times daily use, so the unit isn't regenerating too often.
  3. Plan the location. The softener goes on the main line, usually before the water heater, near a drain and an outlet.
  4. Install. A bypass valve, inlet and outlet connections, a drain line with an air gap, and the brine tank overflow are installed.
  5. Program and start up. The control is set for hardness and household size, and the first regeneration is run.
  6. Test. Treated water is tested at a faucet to confirm it is soft.

Why hard water costs you money

Scale builds up inside water heaters, tankless heat exchangers, faucets, showerheads and appliances. The Department of Energy recommends flushing sediment from storage water heaters regularly, and hard water makes that buildup faster. In tankless water heaters, scale on the heat exchanger cuts performance and leads to error codes. A softener ahead of the heater reduces that buildup.

Types of water treatment

  • Salt-based (ion exchange) softener: the standard choice; it removes hardness minerals.
  • Dual-tank softener: one tank serves water while the other regenerates, for large households.
  • Potassium chloride option: uses potassium instead of sodium.
  • Salt-free conditioner: changes how minerals form scale but does not remove them; results vary.
  • Iron filters and reverse osmosis: for iron, taste or drinking water, often paired with a softener.

What affects the cost of a water softener

  • Capacity and type of unit.
  • Water hardness and iron content.
  • Where it goes, and plumbing changes to reach the main line.
  • A new drain or outlet nearby.
  • Added filters or a drinking water system.

DIY or call a professional?

You can add salt, break up a salt bridge and check the control settings after a power outage. Installation involves cutting into the main water line, adding a bypass and running a drain with a proper air gap, so it is best done by a licensed plumber. Some areas also limit softener discharge to septic systems or sewers; a plumber can check local rules.

Softener maintenance

  • Keep the salt tank at least a quarter full.
  • Check for salt bridges every month or two.
  • Clean the brine tank every year or so.
  • Have the resin and valve checked if water starts feeling hard again.
