Quick answer: Sewer line repair fixes the pipe that carries waste from your house to the public sewer. The plumber first runs a camera through the line to find cracks, roots, offset joints or sagging sections. Then the fix is a spot repair, a full replacement by digging, or a trenchless method such as pipe lining or pipe bursting.

What sewer line repair includes

  • Video camera inspection through a cleanout or the toilet flange, with a recording you can keep.
  • Line locating to mark the pipe's path and depth from the surface.
  • Clearing with a cable machine or hydro jetting before repair or lining.
  • Spot repair of a single broken section or joint.
  • Trenchless options: cured-in-place pipe (CIPP) lining and pipe bursting.
  • Open-trench replacement when the pipe has collapsed or has a long sag (a "belly").
  • Cleanout installation for easier service later.
  • Permits, locates and inspection where your city requires them.

The EPA lists grease, so-called "flushable" wipes, tree roots and leaky or broken sewer pipes among the causes of sewer overflows and backups.

The sewer line repair process

  1. Clear the line. If there is a backup, the plumber opens the line first so the house is usable.
  2. Inspect with a camera. The camera shows the pipe material, the defects and their distance from the access point.
  3. Locate. A locator marks where and how deep the problem is. Utility lines are marked through 811 before any digging.
  4. Choose the method. You get a written quote with options, such as spot repair, lining or replacement.
  5. Permit and repair. The work is done, with an inspection where your city requires one.
  6. Verify. A final camera run shows the repaired line, and the yard or floor is restored as agreed.

Trenchless sewer repair vs. digging

Trenchless methods replace most of a trench with one or two small access pits. That can save driveways, landscaping and slabs.

  • Pipe lining (CIPP): a resin-soaked felt liner is pulled or inverted into the old pipe, then cured to form a new, jointless pipe inside the old one. It needs the host pipe to keep its shape and be cleaned first.
  • Pipe bursting: a bursting head pulls new HDPE pipe through the old line, breaking the old pipe outward as it goes. It works on badly broken pipe and can upsize the line.
  • Open trench: still the right choice for bellies, collapsed sections that a liner can't pass, or very short, shallow repairs.

Not every line qualifies for trenchless work. The camera inspection decides that, not a sales pitch.

Signs you need a sewer camera inspection

  • More than one drain backs up, or the basement floor drain overflows.
  • Toilets gurgle when the washer drains or the tub empties.
  • The same main line clog comes back within months.
  • You smell sewage outside or see a wet, sunken or bright green strip in the yard.
  • You are buying a home built more than 30 years ago.

After a sewage backup

The EPA says areas flooded by sewage must be cleaned and disinfected, and that porous items such as carpet and drywall that got wet may need to be replaced. Keep children and pets away and wear gloves. Take photos for your insurer before cleanup. Large backups usually need a water damage restoration company as well as a plumber.

What affects the cost of sewer line repair

  • Length and depth of the pipe, and what sits over it: lawn, driveway or slab.
  • Pipe material and condition.
  • Repair method: spot repair, lining, bursting or open trench.
  • Permit, inspection and city connection fees.
  • Surface restoration such as concrete or landscaping.

DIY or call a professional?

You can run water in short bursts to test drains and keep grease and wipes out of the line. Sewer repair involves excavation, utility locates, permits and connections to the public sewer, so hire a licensed plumber. Do not open a cleanout when the line is full of sewage; it can spill under pressure. If your main clue is odor, our guide to finding the source of sewer gas in a home covers the simple causes to rule out first.

Prevent sewer line problems

  • Throw wipes, even "flushable" ones, in the trash.
  • Collect cooking grease in a can, not the sink.
  • Plant trees well away from the sewer line's path.
  • Ask about a backwater valve if the city sewer has backed up in your area.
  • Schedule maintenance cleaning if roots are a known problem.
