Quick answer: Leak detection finds water leaks you can't see: inside walls, under floors and slabs, and in the yard line. Plumbers use meter and pressure tests to confirm a leak, then acoustic listening gear, thermal imaging and moisture meters to pinpoint it. The EPA says the average household's leaks can waste about 9,300 gallons a year.

What leak detection includes

  • Meter test to confirm water is moving when nothing is on.
  • Pressure testing to isolate the leak to hot, cold, inside or outside lines.
  • Acoustic detection with ground microphones and listening devices.
  • Thermal imaging to spot hot water leaks and wet areas by temperature.
  • Moisture meters to map how far water has spread in drywall, wood and flooring.
  • Fixture checks for toilets, faucets, valves and the water heater.
  • A written report with the leak location and the repair options.

The leak detection process

  1. Confirm the leak. All fixtures are off and the meter is watched, then lines are pressure tested.
  2. Narrow it down. Valves split the system into zones until the leaking line is found.
  3. Pinpoint. Acoustic and thermal tools find the spot, often within a foot or two.
  4. Mark and report. The location is marked, with photos and a written summary for you and your insurer.
  5. Repair. The plumber quotes the fix: a small opening and spot repair, a reroute, or repiping.

Do-it-yourself leak checks

The EPA's WaterSense program suggests a few simple checks before you call:

  • Meter test: read the water meter, use no water for two hours, then read it again. If it moved, there is probably a leak.
  • Toilet dye test: put a few drops of food coloring in the tank. If color shows in the bowl after 10 minutes without flushing, the flapper is leaking.
  • Winter bill check: the EPA says a family of four using more than 12,000 gallons a month in winter probably has a serious leak.

If a toilet or faucet is the cause, it is usually a quick fix. If the meter still moves with every fixture off, the leak is hidden, and that is when detection pays off.

Signs of a hidden water leak

  • A water bill that rises with no change in use.
  • The sound of running water when everything is off.
  • Warm spots on a tile or concrete floor.
  • Stains, bubbling paint, or soft drywall.
  • Musty smell or mold growth.
  • Low water pressure or a water heater that runs constantly.
  • Wet or sinking spots in the yard between the meter and the house.

Why fast detection matters

A hidden leak keeps soaking framing, insulation and subfloor. The EPA advises drying wet building materials within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold. The Insurance Information Institute notes that many policies cover sudden water damage but not damage from slow, long-term leaks. Finding a leak early limits both the damage and the claim questions.

What affects the cost of leak detection

  • Where the leak may be: walls, slab, ceiling or yard.
  • Size of the home and the number of lines to test.
  • Methods needed, such as tracer gas.
  • After-hours service.
  • Repair work after the leak is found.

DIY or call a professional?

Do the meter and dye tests yourself, and fix a toilet flapper if that is the cause. When the leak is in a wall, a slab or underground, professional detection saves money by keeping the opening small. Guessing and opening walls in several places usually costs more.

Prevent future leaks

  • Check under sinks, behind toilets and around the water heater every few months.
  • Replace washing machine hoses with braided steel hoses every five years or so.
  • Know where your main shutoff is, and test it once a year.
  • Consider a leak sensor or automatic shutoff valve.
  • Keep water pressure in a normal range; a plumber can test it.
